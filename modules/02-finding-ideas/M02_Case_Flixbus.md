# Three Friends and a Promised Market

Updated: 2026-10-19.

> Module 02 · Finding Ideas · Case, read before class

Case for Module 02, Finding Ideas, of Ambitious Entrepreneurship. Prepared by Bastian Burger from public sources as a basis for class discussion. The case does not judge whether any decision was right or wrong. Licensed under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 except third-party material as marked.
{: .byline }

In the summer of 2011 three friends who had worked in Munich shared a wish. Jochen Engert and André Schwämmlein, two strategy consultants who had met at the Boston Consulting Group (BCG), had begun doctorates. Daniel Krauss had worked as a software developer at Microsoft.[^jobs] Each of them had wanted a company of their own.[^wish]

Since 2009 a sentence in the coalition agreement of the governing parties CDU, CSU and FDP had promised to allow scheduled long-distance buses in Germany, which a law of 1934 had restricted.[^rule] By March 2011 the promise had become a draft law.[^draft] In June 2011 Deutsche Bahn, the railway, decided to hold back in long-distance buses for now.[^db]

Engert later described a point at which the three had to decide whether to finish their doctorates and go back into consulting, to stay with their secure jobs, or to make the jump.[^paths] No source says when they reached that point. This case describes what they could have known at the end of June 2011.

## Three friends and a wish

Engert and Schwämmlein had started as strategy consultants at BCG in Munich. Krauss recalled in 2017 that the two met there while playing football, and Schwämmlein recalled in 2018 that he had worked with Engert from his first job.[^met] Schwämmlein also recalled that he and Krauss had gone to the same school and played volleyball together.[^school] Krauss worked at Microsoft in Munich after working in automotive development.[^krauss]

Engert worked on a doctoral thesis on pricing at the WHU Otto Beisheim School of Management in Vallendar, a university magazine reported in 2016. Engert recalled in 2025 that Schwämmlein's thesis was on innovative transport systems.[^phd] The sources differ on how the doctorates and the jobs fitted together. The magazine said Engert took a sabbatical in 2010 to write his thesis. Engert told a newspaper in 2017 that the two began their doctorates alongside their consulting jobs. An interviewer wrote in 2019 that all three were employed when the idea arose.[^status] No source says who worked where on 30 June 2011.

Schwämmlein and Krauss had founded a company together before. The regional startup portal Munich Startup reported in 2017 that they founded it in 2005 and sold it after two years.[^it] Schwämmlein recalled that it was an IT consultancy for mid-sized companies, that it did not scale, because growth would have meant more work for Krauss, and that building something themselves and carrying the responsibility had been great fun.[^it2] Engert recalled in 2025 that as a student he had organised ski trips with a friend, which often filled a bus, and that this was all his experience with buses.[^ski]

Each of the three said later that he wanted to found something. Engert recalled in 2025 that the three sat together from time to time and agreed that they would not stay in consulting forever and wanted to do something entrepreneurial, and that he and Schwämmlein first started doctorates.[^wish2] Schwämmlein recalled in 2018 that all three wanted to found a company and were looking for the right idea, and that he did not want to be an employee or to draw slides in consulting for the rest of his life.[^wish3] Krauss recalled in 2017 that he and Schwämmlein had been self-employed before and wanted to become so again.[^wish4]

## The rule and the promise

Under the Passenger Transport Act of 1934 an operator needed a permit for a long-distance bus line in Germany. The authority had to refuse it where existing transport already served the route satisfactorily, or where the line would take over tasks that existing operators or railways already performed without a significant improvement.[^act] Green members of the federal parliament, the Bundestag, wrote in 2010 that, apart from exceptions such as traffic to and from Berlin and feeder services to airports, Germany had almost no scheduled long-distance bus service. In their view permit authorities usually followed Deutsche Bahn's argument that its own trains served a route and refused applications, and earlier attempts to ease the rules had failed.[^greens]

The coalition agreement of 2009 said, in the sentence the Green members quoted, that the coalition would allow scheduled long-distance bus services and amend section 13 of the Act.[^coal] On 6 July 2010 the federal government answered the Greens' questions. It said it expected the market for long-distance bus lines to develop and called coaches a sensible means of transport, economically and ecologically. It pointed to a ruling of the Federal Administrative Court of 24 June 2010, which in its words had already loosened the protection against competition considerably, because a permit authority could give decisive weight to much lower bus fares.[^gov] It said that, beyond a sensitivity analysis within the review of the federal rail needs plan, which was to estimate what allowing long-distance buses would mean for the plan's cost-benefit study, no further study of liberalisation scenarios had been carried out.[^study] Asked what toll buses would pay, it gave the road-use costs that buses caused on federal motorways, taken from a study of 2007, as EUR 0.09 per kilometre in 2010 and EUR 0.10 in 2012, without separating long-distance buses from regional ones (Exhibit 2).[^toll]

By March 2011 a draft law to liberalise long-distance buses was in consultation. On 11 March the Verbraucherzentrale Bundesverband, the federation of consumer organisations, welcomed it.[^fed1] The federation wrote that a coach was on average a third cheaper than the train and therefore attractive to travellers with little money, and that it let older travellers go from point to point without changing. It reported that Deutsche Bahn estimated that long-distance rail would lose about three percent of its passengers after liberalisation, and it concluded that train and coach served different groups. The train, it wrote, offered more comfort and good working conditions on board and was mostly faster, while the coach was limited in punctuality and speed by the risk of traffic jams but clearly cheaper.[^fed2] The federation wanted the permit for a coach operator limited to the operator's safety and reliability. It criticised that the draft kept an operating obligation, the duty to keep running the line for the whole term of the permit, and the approval of timetables and fares. It wanted the ban on duplicating regional rail services limited to local transport, because the ban would otherwise force authorities to refuse a coach line if regional rail lost even a few passengers, though it called the ban of little practical importance for coaches, which hardly competed with regional rail. The federation wanted coaches to pay a toll for road use, like the charges trains paid for track and stations, and suggested examining an operator-neutral information platform and sales platform for all long-distance bus trips.[^fed3]

Long-distance rail carried 126 million passengers in Germany in 2010, 2.4 percent more than in 2009, according to the Federal Statistical Office, and the average long-distance trip by bus or rail was 290 kilometres (Exhibit 2).[^stat] Engert recalled in 2025 that a Deutsche Bahn subsidiary had run buses between Hamburg and Berlin 14 or 15 times a day while long-distance lines were still restricted, and that this was widely taken for the size of the market.[^hh] He also recalled that the three had doubted for a long time whether the law would really come and whether the politicians would liberalise.[^doubt]

## Deutsche Bahn and DeinBus

Deutsche Bahn ran buses too. In August 2009 it started its first international long-distance coach line, between Nuremberg and Prague, on a link where it had stopped running trains about five years earlier.[^np] A rail passengers' association reported that Deutsche Bahn had announced in August 2010 that the line had carried about 120,000 passengers in a year.[^np2] In June 2011 a news agency reported that a planned market offensive in long-distance buses had been called off because of the uncertainties of the coming liberalisation, and that Deutsche Bahn said it would for now make no multi-million investment in expanding its bus lines. According to the report, board member Ulrich Homburg said that Deutsche Bahn took no unnecessary risks and concentrated on its core business, and that for now it would not engage beyond its existing regional and long-distance bus activities. The agency wrote that Deutsche Bahn then ran more than 50 long-distance bus lines.[^dbrep]

DeinBus was a bus car-pooling service founded by three students. It organised long-distance coach trips and, according to a news agency, competed with Deutsche Bahn for long-distance passengers. Deutsche Bahn lost a case against it in April 2011. In May a Deutsche Bahn spokesman said that the railway would not appeal and added that the government planned a new law that would liberalise long-distance transport.[^dein]

The three described Deutsche Bahn's stance in different ways (Exhibit 3). Engert said in 2016 that they had taken it for granted that Deutsche Bahn would take the market itself, and that it did not find the market attractive. The magazine wrote that Deutsche Bahn turned up its nose at the idea.[^db1] Schwämmlein said in 2018 that a member of Deutsche Bahn's board had said that bus traffic did not interest him, after which the three took up the project again.[^db2] In another 2018 interview he recalled that Deutsche Bahn at some point said it did not find the business exciting and did not believe in buses.[^db3] He added that they had been unsure at first about entering, because the business lived on offering many routes and on being the largest provider, and a firm like Deutsche Bahn had every precondition for that.[^db4] Engert recalled in 2025 that the three had feared that Deutsche Bahn would put several hundred buses on the road and leave no room for others, that the board then said it did not find the market interesting, and that the three became attentive again.[^db5] The business weekly WirtschaftsWoche reported in 2016 that a Deutsche Bahn announcement of 2011 not to expand its own bus business was, in Schwämmlein's words, the spark they needed.[^db6] The three did not name the board member, and no source dates the remark they meant. Schwämmlein's magazine account of 2018 places it after a pause in 2009. Engert in 2025 and WirtschaftsWoche in 2016 place it in 2011, and the other accounts give no date.

## Looking for an idea

Schwämmlein recalled in 2018 that the three searched for an idea together, ideally in a market in which digitalisation had so far played no big role, and that they came across the deregulation of the long-distance bus market. Two things appealed to him. The business was analogue, so digitalisation could enter, and deregulation changed the market structure and created new possibilities. He called it a search for an idea for digitalisation.[^idea1] Krauss said in 2017 that the team had collected thousands of ideas, from knitted socks to online portals, before the news of the planned law arrived.[^idea2] WirtschaftsWoche wrote that Schwämmlein and Engert had kept a list that grew longer and had run business models for 50 products and services.[^idea3] The sources name no idea on the list beyond those examples, and they contain no score, sheet or market analysis from the time.

Krauss recalled in 2017 that it had not mattered much what the business was, that the three knew little about buses, and that it was important for it to be not just another app or website but something tangible with an impact.[^idea4] Engert recalled in 2025 that the old-fashioned market of mid-sized family firms, which had built good businesses over generations, appealed to him, that the three felt they could add value with a platform, a brand and technology, and that a liberalisation was a rare chance.[^idea5]

How the three came across the plan was told in several ways (Exhibit 3). Engert recalled in 2025 a half-sentence in the coalition agreement of 2009 that they read and found interesting, and that they were then deep in consulting and had little time to look around.[^come1] Schwämmlein recalled in 2018 that the project rested for about nine months in 2009, because they feared that the liberalisation might not come.[^come2] Krauss was quoted in 2017 as saying that Schwämmlein came one day with the news that the law was to be loosened.[^come3] WirtschaftsWoche wrote that after a ski holiday in 2010 Schwämmlein and Engert remembered that the government had announced liberalisations in regulated industries, and added long-distance coaches to their list.[^come4]

Engert said in 2017 that a market analysis had shown 4,500 bus companies in Germany, mostly mid-sized, so that a business built on digitalisation, a strong brand and a central online sales platform could succeed.[^an1] Krauss recalled in 2021, hedging, that there had been almost 5,000 bus companies when they started (Exhibit 2).[^an2] The sources contain no other figure from the analysis. Engert recalled in 2025 that car-pooling offers were for the three at first a data source for which routes looked good and carried many offers, and a sign that demand existed. The sources do not say when they used them.[^pool] They also discussed how to organise the business, according to Engert, from buying buses and hiring drivers, to an online platform alone, to operators who ran the buses while the three supplied platform, technology, marketing and brand. The sources do not date the discussion.[^models]

The sources differ on when preparation began. Munich Startup reported in 2017 that thoughts about the project began to take shape in 2010 and that the three founded a civil-law partnership in 2011, without a month.[^prep3] Engert placed the first talks with bus operators in 2011, without a month.[^prep1] Two other accounts of the length of the preparation imply starts around mid-2010 and in the second half of 2011.[^prep2] The sources do not say whether any talk with an operator took place before 30 June 2011. Apart from the three themselves, they contain no statement by a traveller, and no paper that the three wrote at the time.

## Finish, stay or jump

Engert recalled in 2016 that the three reached a point at which they had to decide whether to finish their doctorates and then go back into consulting, to stay with their secure jobs, or to make the jump and start a company.[^pt1] In 2025 he described it as the point at which one jumps or does not.[^pt2] No source says which path applied to whom. Only Engert and Schwämmlein are reported to have had doctorates under way and consulting careers, and Krauss had worked at Microsoft.

Family, friends and partners, Engert recalled in 2025, called the idea crazy, because the two had good consulting jobs and could finish their doctorates and go back.[^crazy] He gave his own reason for the jump. If the three did not do it and someone else did and it worked, they would regret it for the rest of their lives, and he had said, come on, let's jump.[^regret]

## Summer 2011

At the end of June 2011 the promise of the coalition agreement of 2009 stood, a draft law was in consultation, and Deutsche Bahn had decided to hold back in long-distance buses for now. Engert, Schwämmlein and Krauss each had a path in front of him, a wish for a company of their own and a market that the government had promised to open. Engert later described the question as the point at which one jumps or does not.
